CASE 01 / BREATHE EASY SOLUTION

When booking emails did not reach customers.

Software troubleshooting

A customer should receive a confirmation after submitting a service booking. In this project, that email sometimes failed to arrive, so I investigated the code and email API used by the booking flow.

01 / PROBLEM

Confirmation did not arrive

Some customers submitted a booking but did not receive the expected confirmation email.

02 / POSSIBLE CAUSES

Where the failure could occur

The booking code might not call the email API correctly; the recipient address or request data could be wrong; or the email service could reject or filter a message. These were possibilities to investigate, not confirmed root causes.

03 / DIAGNOSIS

Checked the sending path

I reviewed the email API integration and the program logic responsible for sending customer booking confirmations, looking for why the message did not consistently reach the customer.

04 / SOLUTION

Corrected the integration

I fixed the issue in the email-related code and API setup. Customer booking confirmations began arriving again.

05 / LESSON LEARNED

Check the complete customer journey

A submitted booking and a delivered confirmation are separate outcomes. The email step needs its own checks so a sending failure can be noticed and resolved quickly.

Case 01 of 05 documented · Additional software cases will be added from completed troubleshooting work.

02Cybersecurity case studyProtecting public inquiry forms.+
Defensive case study · 2-page document
BREATHE EASY SOLUTION / FORM PROTECTION

Responding to suspicious inquiries.

Defensive case study

After the website went live, I received an inquiry that appeared to be automated. It prompted me to strengthen the front-end checks on the quotation, product, and contact forms with better field validation and submission limits.

OBSERVEDA suspicious inquiry reached the site.

The message raised a concern about possible form spam; it was not proof of a confirmed intrusion.

RESPONSEProtected three public forms.

I improved field validation and limited repeated submissions across the quotation, product, and contact forms.

01 / THE ISSUE

What was the security concern?

A public inquiry form can attract repeated or unwanted submissions. The suspicious message suggested possible automated form abuse, so I treated it as a warning sign and reviewed all three customer-facing forms.

02 / HOW IT HAPPENS

How could a bot submit inquiries?

Scripts can send requests to a public form repeatedly, often faster than a person would. Browser-side checks improve the experience but can be bypassed if the server does not enforce its own rules.

03 / THE RISK

Why does it matter?

Spam can bury genuine customer requests, fill inquiry records, and generate unnecessary notification emails. A busy inbox can delay a response to a real quotation or product question.

04 / MY RESPONSE

What did I change?

I strengthened field validation and added submission rate limits to the quotation, product, and contact forms. These front-end improvements help reject invalid entries and slow repeated attempts while keeping the forms usable for customers.

05 / CONTINUING PRACTICE

How should the protection be maintained?

Form rules and rate limits should be enforced where submissions are processed, then checked against real traffic so legitimate customers are not blocked. Suspicious patterns can be monitored without publishing customer messages or personal details.

PERSONAL REFLECTION / MY JOURNEY IN BSIT

From knowing computers to building solutions.

The projects, questions, and setbacks that changed how I see software development.

Before I studied BSIT, I understood it in a broad way. I thought the course was mostly about computers: learning hardware and software, fixing problems, and helping people when something stopped working. I still value those skills, but programming changed my perspective. When I began creating simple websites and applications, I realized that IT is also about making something new from an idea. A working application needs more than code on a screen. It needs a purpose, a plan, and decisions about how people will use it. That shift—from seeing computers mainly as things to repair to seeing software as something I could build—made me take the course more seriously.

One of my turning points came while I was working on an early website project at Olivarez College, my first school. I was just starting to see myself as a software developer. AI tools existed then, but I did not have the kind of assistance that can now produce a page quickly. I had to think through the design, plan what belonged on the site, and work through development problems myself. It was difficult, and it showed me that a website does not appear simply because someone knows a programming language. I had to connect the design, the functions, and the needs behind the project. That experience made me realize that this course was no joke, but it also made me want to keep building.

As I worked on more websites and applications, a different challenge became clearer: understanding what a customer or client really needs. A client might describe what they want, but I still have to ask questions, understand the people who will use the product, and decide how the site or application can help them. I am also learning that technical work needs communication. It is not enough to have a skill; I need to explain what I can do, show my work, and help someone see the value of a solution. Thinking about both the user and the client has stretched me beyond simply finishing a feature.

Programming remains one of my biggest areas for improvement. I learned its foundations during my first two years in college, yet I still find some parts difficult. AI has made my development process faster, and I appreciate that help. At the same time, speed is not the same as understanding. I want to become more confident reading the code, explaining why it works, and fixing it when the result is wrong. That takes continued practice, patience, and a willingness to revisit the basics instead of relying only on a tool to finish the task for me.

I also want to improve how I think through design and problem solving. Sometimes I choose an approach that seems right, then the next day I see a better one. I used to think a changing solution meant I had failed to plan properly. Now I see that the first idea can be a starting point, as long as I test it against the real need and improve it for a reason. I want to get better at identifying the problem before jumping to a design, listening carefully to feedback, and knowing when a revision actually helps the customer.

I feel ready to begin an IT career, but I do not think graduating will automatically guarantee a job. The field is competitive, and being ready means continuing to sharpen my technical skills, my logic, and my mindset. My early projects taught me how demanding development can be; client work taught me to think beyond my own preferences; and setbacks reminded me that I still have a lot to learn. I am open to feedback and willing to keep improving. For me, readiness is not claiming that I know everything. It is being prepared to contribute, to ask better questions, and to grow through the work ahead.

WHAT I AM TAKING FORWARDBuild with purpose. Understand the code. Keep improving.

What kinds of projects can you help with?+

I can help create websites for businesses and personal projects, from presenting your services and showcasing your work to handling customer inquiries and supporting everyday operations. We can discuss your goals and choose the features that will be most useful to you.

Can you improve an existing website or system?+

Yes. I start by reviewing how your current website or system works, what it already does well, and where it falls short. I then suggest practical improvements, which may include new features, clearer interfaces, stronger field validation, rate limiting, or other security measures suited to the project.

What information do you need to start a project?+

I first need to understand what the project is for, who will use it, and what you want it to improve. The details depend on whether it is a business or personal project. If your goal is to attract customers or automate work, we can review your current process and identify the features that would best support it.

Why are some projects shown through screenshots rather than a live demo?+

Client and customer privacy comes first. Some projects involve private systems or information that should not be publicly accessible, so I share only screenshots or demonstrations that I am permitted to show. Whether a live demo is available also depends on the project and its hosting setup.

Can you help with updates after a project is completed?+

Yes. I can help maintain a website or system, fix issues, improve its performance, and add features as your needs develop. We can agree on the scope and schedule of ongoing support so it is clear what work is included.
